U.S. food-label basics · Allergen statements

“Contains” vs. “May Contain”: what do they mean on food labels?

You may see both statements on packaged food, but they do not mean the same thing. One identifies major food allergens used as ingredients. The other is a voluntary warning about the possibility of unintended allergen cross-contact.

Key difference: “Contains” is used to declare major food allergens that are ingredients in the product. “May contain” is a voluntary advisory statement about a possible unintended presence, often related to shared equipment or production conditions. Neither statement replaces reading the full label.

1. What does “Contains” mean?

For most packaged foods regulated by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA), federal law requires the food source of each major food allergen used to make the food to be identified on the label. The source name can be included in the ingredient name itself, shown in parentheses after an ingredient, or declared in a separate “Contains” statement placed immediately after or next to the ingredient list.

For example, the source may be clear from an ingredient name such as “buttermilk.” Other examples include:

  • Whey (milk)
  • Flour (wheat)
  • Lecithin (soy)

A separate statement might say “Contains: Wheat, Milk, and Soy.” If a Contains statement is present, it must identify all major food allergens used as ingredients in that product. A product containing a major allergen does not always have a separate Contains statement, so the ingredient list must still be checked.

2. What are the nine major food allergens in the United States?

U.S. law identifies nine major food allergens for FDA-regulated foods:

  1. Milk
  2. Eggs
  3. Fish (the species must be identified)
  4. Crustacean shellfish (the species must be identified)
  5. Tree nuts (the specific tree nut must be identified)
  6. Peanuts
  7. Wheat
  8. Soybeans
  9. Sesame

Sesame became the ninth major food allergen on January 1, 2023. FDA guidance on tree nuts was updated in its fifth-edition allergen guidance in 2025; for example, coconut is no longer included in FDA's list of tree nuts considered major food allergens. Coconut used as an ingredient may still need to be declared in the ingredient list under the applicable ingredient-naming rules. Do not treat the major-allergen list as a complete list of every food that can cause an allergy.

3. What does “May Contain” mean?

“May contain [allergen]” is an example of an allergen advisory statement. Manufacturers may use it when there is a possibility that an allergen could unintentionally get into a food during manufacturing—for example, when different products are made on shared equipment or on the same production line.

This unintended transfer is called cross-contact. A small amount of an allergen-containing ingredient from another product may remain on equipment or enter a product during production, even when cleaning and other controls are used.

Other wording can include “made on shared equipment” or “produced in a facility that also handles [allergen].” These are advisory statements, not the same as listing an allergen as an intentional ingredient.

4. Is “May Contain” required by law?

No. The FDA explains that allergen advisory statements such as “may contain” are voluntary and are not required by the major food allergen labeling law. Manufacturers do not all use the same advisory wording, and not all products with a possible cross-contact concern carry such a statement.

That means the absence of a “May Contain” warning does not prove that cross-contact is impossible or that the food is suitable for someone with an allergy. FDA guidance also says an advisory statement is not a substitute for appropriate food-safety controls and must be truthful and not misleading.

5. See the difference in a fictional label

Fictional chocolate cookie package

Ingredients: Wheat flour, sugar, butter, chocolate chips (sugar, chocolate, cocoa butter, soy lecithin (soy)), cocoa powder, salt, natural flavor.

Contains: Wheat, Milk, Soy.

May contain: Peanuts.

This is an invented teaching example, not a real product label.

In this example:

  • Wheat, milk and soy are shown as major food allergens used as ingredients in the recipe.
  • Peanuts appear only in the voluntary advisory statement, indicating a possible unintended cross-contact concern—not an intended recipe ingredient.
  • The example does not tell you the probability or amount of any cross-contact. The words alone are not a measurement of risk.

6. Why you should read the whole label every time

Do not rely only on a bold Contains line. Read the full ingredient statement too, including ingredient names in parentheses and compound ingredients. The allergen source may be apparent from a common ingredient name or explained in the list.

Check the package each time you buy a product, even if you have bought it before. Manufacturers can change ingredients and production processes. The FDA recommends contacting the manufacturer or distributor shown on the package when you are unsure about whether a product contains an allergen.

Important safety point: If you or someone you care for has a food allergy, do not use the absence of a “May Contain” statement as proof that a product is safe. When the label is unclear, do not guess; contact the manufacturer or distributor and follow advice from your qualified healthcare professional.

7. Which foods are covered by these FDA rules?

The FDA's major-allergen labeling requirements apply to most FDA-regulated packaged foods and dietary supplements. Some products are regulated by other U.S. agencies or have different labeling arrangements. For example, meat, poultry and certain egg products are generally regulated by the U.S. Department of Agriculture (USDA), not the FDA. Unpackaged foods and foods prepared after a customer's order can also have different labeling requirements.

This article is a general guide to common U.S. packaged-food labels. Do not assume every food sold in the United States follows exactly the same label format.

Common questions

If a package says “Contains milk,” does that mean milk is an ingredient?

The Contains statement is used to identify major food allergens used as ingredients. Treat the statement as a declaration of the allergen source, and read the full ingredient list for the specific ingredients.

Does “May contain peanuts” mean peanuts are definitely in the product?

No. It is a voluntary advisory that a product may have unintended peanut cross-contact. It does not mean peanuts are an intended ingredient, and the wording does not quantify the likelihood or amount.

If there is no “May Contain” statement, does that mean there is no cross-contact risk?

No. Advisory statements are voluntary, and manufacturers do not all use them. The absence of the statement is not proof that unintended allergen presence is impossible.

Can a label say both “Contains milk” and “May contain milk”?

FDA guidance says it is not appropriate to use a Contains declaration and an advisory statement for the same allergen on the same product, because the statements communicate inconsistent information about whether that allergen is an intended ingredient or an unintended possible presence.

Does this “Big 9” list cover every food allergy?

No. The list identifies the nine major allergens under U.S. federal law for FDA-regulated foods. Other foods can also cause allergic reactions. A person with a food allergy should follow their own medical advice and check labels for the ingredients relevant to them.
