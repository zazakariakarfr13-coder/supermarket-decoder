How to compare two Nutrition Facts labels

Two packages can look similar on the shelf but show different numbers on the back. A fair comparison starts with serving size, then looks at the nutrients that matter to your decision—not one number in isolation.

The first rule: Compare like with like. Check the serving size and the gram amount before comparing calories or nutrient values. If the serving amounts differ, adjust the figures to the same amount of food or choose products with comparable serving sizes.

1. Start with the serving size

The Nutrition Facts panel generally lists calories and nutrients per serving. Look at both the familiar measure (such as cups or pieces) and the metric amount in grams. A “one cup” serving may not weigh the same across two products.

Also check servings per container. The label's serving size is based on the amount people typically eat or drink; it is not a recommendation for how much you should eat. If you eat two servings, you generally consume twice the listed calories and nutrients.

2. Compare similar products using the same amount

For this worked example, imagine two fictional oat cereals. Both labels use the same serving basis: 1 cup (40 g). That makes it easier to compare the listed figures directly. These numbers are invented for learning and do not represent real brands or products.

3. Look at calories—but keep the serving in view

Example A lists 150 calories and Example B lists 160 calories for the same 40 g serving. That is a straightforward comparison because the serving amounts match. If the serving sizes were different, the calorie figures would not be directly comparable until you adjusted them to the same amount.

The 2,000-calorie figure printed on many U.S. labels is a general reference for nutrition advice. Individual calorie needs vary; it is not a personal target for everyone.

4. Use % Daily Value one nutrient at a time

The % Daily Value (%DV) tells you how much a nutrient in one serving contributes to a general daily reference amount. The FDA's general guide is:

  • 5% DV or less is considered low for that nutrient per serving.
  • 20% DV or more is considered high for that nutrient per serving.

Use %DV to compare individual nutrients, not to create a combined score for the whole product. For example, Example A has 13% DV sodium, while Example B has 10% DV. Example A has 14% DV dietary fiber, while Example B has 11% DV. Those are separate pieces of information, not a verdict.

5. Choose which nutrients to compare

Depending on the product and what you are looking for, you might compare:

  • Sodium: compare the milligrams and %DV. The FDA's Daily Value for sodium is 2,300 mg.
  • Saturated fat: compare the grams and %DV. The Daily Value is 20 g.
  • Added Sugars: check the amount and %DV. The Daily Value is 50 g.
  • Dietary fiber: compare grams and %DV. The Daily Value is 28 g.

For nutrients you are generally advised to get less of—such as sodium, saturated fat and Added Sugars—you may look for lower values. For nutrients many people are encouraged to get more of, such as dietary fiber, a higher value may be useful. The right comparison depends on the food, your circumstances and your preferences.

Not every line has a %DV. For example, Total Sugars does not have a %DV on the U.S. Nutrition Facts label. Protein %DV is shown only in specific circumstances. Read the grams as well as the percentage when a percentage is provided.

6. Read the ingredient list after the numbers

The Nutrition Facts panel shows calories and selected nutrient amounts. The ingredient list identifies the ingredients used to make the product. A comparison is more complete when you look at both.

Check the first ingredients, look inside compound ingredients where shown, and notice sweetening ingredients or allergens that matter to you. Our companion guide explains how to read ingredient lists on U.S. food labels. You can also learn to recognise different names for added-sugar ingredients.

7. What does the example actually tell us?

Example A: fewer calories in the matched serving and more dietary fiber, but more sodium and more Added Sugars than Example B.

Example B: slightly more calories and less fiber in the matched serving, but less sodium and fewer Added Sugars.

What we cannot conclude: that one cereal is automatically “healthy” or “unhealthy.” The comparison only shows differences in the selected label values. Ingredients, other nutrients, price, taste, allergies and personal needs can also matter.

A quick supermarket comparison checklist

  1. Are these products in the same category?
  2. Do the serving sizes and gram amounts match?
  3. Have I compared calories for the same amount?
  4. Which specific nutrients do I want to compare?
  5. Have I checked both the %DV and the actual grams or milligrams?
  6. Have I read the ingredients and checked anything relevant to my needs?
