How to read an ingredient list on U.S. food labels

The ingredient list tells you what goes into a packaged food. Learn to read it in order, notice ingredients inside other ingredients, and use the list alongside—not instead of—the Nutrition Facts panel.

Start here: Find the word “Ingredients,” read from the beginning, and scan the entire list. In general, ingredients are listed in descending order of predominance by weight: the ingredient used in the greatest amount comes first.

1. Find the ingredient statement

On many packaged foods, the ingredient statement appears near the Nutrition Facts panel or elsewhere on the information panel. Look for a heading such as “Ingredients”. It is a list of the ingredients declared for the product, using common or usual names under U.S. food-labeling rules.

The list answers a different question from the Nutrition Facts panel. The ingredient statement identifies what ingredients are used; the Nutrition Facts panel gives amounts of energy and selected nutrients. Read both when you want a fuller picture of a packaged food.

2. Read the order—but do not stop after the first three

For U.S. packaged foods, ingredients are generally listed in descending order of predominance by weight. The first ingredient is generally present in a greater amount by weight than the ingredients that follow it. The order can give you a quick sense of how the product is formulated.

Fictional example: oat-and-cranberry snack

Ingredients: Whole grain oats, cane sugar, sunflower oil, dried cranberries (cranberries, sugar, sunflower oil), sea salt, natural flavor.

  1. Whole grain oats appear first in this example, so they are the most predominant listed ingredient by weight.
  2. Cane sugar and sunflower oil follow. Their position gives context, but the list does not disclose their exact percentages.
  3. Dried cranberries include their own component ingredients in parentheses in this illustrative example.
  4. Sea salt and natural flavor appear later in the statement. Their position alone does not tell you the complete nutritional effect of the food.

This is a made-up example for learning, not a real product label.

The first three ingredients are a useful starting point, not a complete evaluation. Keep reading: an ingredient farther down the list may still matter for an allergy, preference or other personal reason.

3. Look inside compound ingredients

Some ingredients are themselves made from several components. These are sometimes called compound ingredients or sub-ingredients. A label may show the components in parentheses after the compound ingredient's name—for example, “dried cranberries (cranberries, sugar, sunflower oil)”.

Do not skip the parentheses. They can reveal additional ingredients that are part of a sauce, coating, seasoning blend, filling or other prepared component. U.S. labeling rules also allow certain sub-ingredients to be declared within the overall ingredient statement in other ways, so the presentation can vary.

4. Understand grouped terms such as “spices” and “flavors”

You may see terms such as “spices,” “natural flavor” or “artificial flavor” instead of a list of every individual flavoring component. U.S. rules permit some ingredients to be declared using these collective terms. Their appearance does not, by itself, tell you the exact recipe or whether the product is nutritious.

Color additives have their own declaration rules. For example, FDA-certified color additives must be identified by their specified names or permitted abbreviated names. Other color additives may be declared differently. Avoid assuming that every color on a label follows the same naming convention.

5. Recognize different names for sweetening ingredients

Sweetening ingredients do not always appear under the single word “sugar.” Depending on the product, you may encounter names such as cane sugar, glucose, dextrose, fructose, corn syrup or high-fructose corn syrup. Some ingredients have other technological roles as well, so interpret them in the context of the full recipe.

For a closer look at common names, continue to Five names to recognise when scanning food labels. Then check the Added Sugars line on the U.S. Nutrition Facts panel; the ingredient list and the nutrient declaration provide different information.

6. Do not treat the ingredient list as a health score

A longer ingredient list is not automatically a sign that a food is harmful, and a short list does not automatically make a product the right choice for everyone. Ingredients can serve different purposes: some provide structure, texture, color, flavor or freshness. The FDA notes that ingredients must meet the applicable safety standards whether they are naturally or artificially derived.

Use the ingredient list to understand the formulation and identify ingredients that matter to you. For comparison, also consider serving size, calories, nutrients, price, product type, allergies and your own preferences.

7. A simple ingredient-list routine

  1. Find “Ingredients.” Make sure you are reading the list for the product you are holding.
  2. Read from the beginning. Notice which ingredients lead the list, remembering that order is generally by weight.
  3. Open the brackets. Read the components listed inside compound ingredients.
  4. Notice terms you want to understand. For example, syrups, sweeteners, flavors, spices and additives.
  5. Scan the whole statement. Do not rely only on the first three ingredients.
  6. Check the Nutrition Facts panel too. Use it to understand calories and nutrient amounts per serving.

Common questions

Does the first ingredient weigh the most?

Generally, yes. U.S. ingredient statements are normally arranged in descending order of predominance by weight. There are specific regulatory exceptions and special declaration rules, so the list is not a way to calculate exact ingredient percentages.

Does a later ingredient mean it is unimportant?

No. An ingredient's position tells you about its relative weight in the formulation, not whether it matters for an allergy, preference or other individual concern. Read the entire statement.

Why are some ingredients inside parentheses?

Parentheses can identify the components of a compound ingredient, such as a filling, seasoning or prepared fruit ingredient. Read those components as part of the overall ingredient statement.

Is “natural flavor” a complete description of the recipe?

No. It is a permitted collective declaration for certain flavor ingredients. Its presence does not describe the full recipe or determine whether the food is a good fit for you.

Is the ingredient list enough to compare two products?

It is one useful part of the comparison, but not the whole picture. Compare similar products using the Nutrition Facts panel and serving sizes as well. Our U.S. Nutrition Facts label guide explains serving size, calories, sodium, Added Sugars and % Daily Value.
