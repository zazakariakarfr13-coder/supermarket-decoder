Mina wanted six things. Tariq wanted cereal with a cartoon animal on the box. Uncle Kamil wanted to explain the chemistry of breakfast. Nobody had agreed to this last part.

SCENE 03 / THE INGREDIENT LIST

Meet the actual ingredients.

Read the cast list
Uncle Kamil
HMM…

INGREDIENTS

Whole-grain oats, sugar, corn syrup, vegetable oil, cocoa, salt, natural flavour.

Uncle Kamil

In the U.S., ingredients are generally listed in descending order by weight. The first few are the largest ingredients by weight, but the full list still matters.

Mina

Oats, sugar, corn syrup. So sugar brought a friend. And the friend brought syrup.

Tariq

But oats are first. That's good, right?

Uncle Kamil

It's useful information, not a complete health score. Keep reading. A list is a clue, not a verdict.

Label lesson

In U.S. packaged foods, ingredients are generally listed from the greatest to the smallest amount by weight. Check the first few, then read the whole list.

Tariq

Eight servings in one box? Who is this cereal feeding, a book club?

Uncle Kamil

The label numbers are shown per serving. If you eat two servings, you take in roughly twice the listed amounts.

Mina

And the serving size is not a command from the cereal government. It's the reference amount used for the panel.

Tariq

So if I eat two bowls, the calories and sugars don't stay politely in the first bowl?

Uncle Kamil

Correct. The maths follows the food. The food does not follow your optimism.

Label lesson

Check both “servings per container” and “serving size”. Compare the serving on the label with the portion you actually eat.

SCENE 05 / SUGAR'S MANY ALIASES

Sugar has a surprisingly large family.

Alias investigation
Mina
PLINK! PLINK!
10 g added sugar ≈ 2½ teaspoons

ALSO LOOK FOR

Sugar · dextrose · glucose syrup · honey · malt syrup

Mina

Why does sugar have so many aliases? Is it in witness protection?

Uncle Kamil

Different sweeteners have different names. Honey and syrups can still contribute added sugars. The Nutrition Facts panel helps you see the total.

Tariq

Ten grams. That sounds tiny.

Uncle Kamil

Four grams is roughly one teaspoon. Ten grams is about two and a half teaspoons. A small number can look different when you put it in a spoon.

Mina

That's enough teaspoons to start a very disappointing tea party.

Label lesson

On U.S. Nutrition Facts labels, “Includes Added Sugars” is the line to check for added sugars. About 4 grams is roughly one teaspoon; treat it as a visual estimate.

SCENE 06 / THE ADDITIVE RUMOUR MILL

Not every long word is a villain.

Science, not panic
Uncle Kamil
E-WHAT?
E300AntioxidantE471EmulsifierE440ThickenerE160aColour
Tariq

These codes look like passwords for a secret snack society.

Uncle Kamil

In the European system, an E-number identifies an approved food additive. The code is an identifier, not a danger rating.

Mina

So E-number does not automatically mean “run away screaming”?

Uncle Kamil

No. Additives can help preserve food, keep mixtures stable, add colour, or change texture. The useful question is what the ingredient does and whether it's appropriate in that product.

Tariq

So we read, understand, and decide. No dramatic courtroom?

Mina

There is a courtroom. It's called the kitchen table, and I'm the judge.

Label lesson

An additive's name or code alone does not tell you whether the whole product is nutritious. Look at its function and the full label; naming systems differ by country.

Pocket guide · save these rules

Six clues for your next supermarket trip.

01 · FRONTClaims are marketing. Use them as a prompt to investigate, not as proof.
02 · INGREDIENTSCheck the first few ingredients and read the complete list.
03 · SERVINGLook at serving size and servings per container before comparing numbers.
04 · SUGARSCheck total sugars and added sugars; names like syrups and dextrose are useful clues.
05 · ADDITIVESLook at the function and the whole product. A code is not a health verdict.
06 · CONTEXTCompare similar products and consider nutrition, taste, budget and your needs.
