“Contains” vs “May contain”

They look similar, but they answer two different questions. One tells you what is in the recipe. The other is a warning about what might get in by accident.

“Contains” — it is an ingredient

On U.S. packaged foods, the major food allergens used as ingredients must be declared. There are nine: milk, eggs, fish, crustacean shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, soybeans and sesame.

A manufacturer can declare them in one of two ways:

  • a “Contains” statement next to the ingredient list, for example Contains: milk, wheat; or
  • inside the ingredient list itself, for example whey (milk) or flour (wheat).

So no “Contains” line does not mean no allergens. Always read the full ingredient list too.

“May contain” — a possible cross-contact warning

Wording such as “May contain peanuts” or “Made in a facility that also processes tree nuts” is a precautionary (advisory) statement. It refers to possible unintended cross-contact during production, not to an ingredient in the recipe.

In the U.S., this wording is voluntary: manufacturers choose whether to use it, and the wording varies. It must be truthful, but it is not a standardised test result.

No “May contain” line does not prove a food is free from cross-contact.

Shelf-side routine

  1. Read the full ingredient list, including words in brackets.
  2. Check the “Contains” statement if there is one.
  3. Read any “May contain” / facility wording, exactly as written.
  4. Re-check familiar products: recipes and factories change.
  5. If you are unsure, contact the manufacturer.
Outside the U.S.: rules differ. In the EU, 14 allergens must be emphasised (for example in bold) inside the ingredient list, and “may contain” wording is also voluntary. Follow the rules where you shop.

Important: this page is general education, not medical advice. If you or someone you shop for has a food allergy, follow the plan agreed with a doctor or allergist.
