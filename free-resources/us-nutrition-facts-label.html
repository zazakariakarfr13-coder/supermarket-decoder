How to read a Nutrition Facts label

Use this five-step guide to understand the Nutrition Facts panel on packaged foods in the United States. Check the serving first, then use calories and nutrient values to compare similar products.

The quick rule: Check how much is in one serving, look at calories, then use % Daily Value (%DV) to see whether a nutrient amount is low or high. Read the ingredient list too: the Nutrition Facts panel and ingredients list answer different questions.

1. Check serving size and servings per container

Serving size is usually near the top of the Nutrition Facts panel. It is shown in a familiar measure, such as cups, pieces or slices, often followed by a weight in grams. The number of servings per container tells you how many of those servings are in the package.

Nutrition amounts on the panel are generally shown per serving. The serving size reflects the amount people typically eat or drink; it is not a recommendation for how much you should consume.

Ask yourself: “Am I eating one serving, two servings, or the whole package?” That changes the calories and nutrient amounts you actually get.

2. Read calories per serving

Calories measure the energy provided by one serving. Check the serving size before comparing calorie numbers: a product with fewer calories per serving may use a smaller serving size.

Your calorie needs can be higher or lower than the general 2,000-calorie reference used on U.S. labels. It varies from person to person, so do not treat 2,000 calories as an individual target.

3. Understand what 5% and 20% Daily Value mean

The % Daily Value (%DV) shows how much one serving contributes to the Daily Value for a nutrient. It helps you interpret the amount without having to calculate the percentage yourself.

  • 5% DV or less is considered low for that nutrient in one serving.
  • 20% DV or more is considered high for that nutrient in one serving.

For nutrients you are trying to limit, such as sodium, saturated fat and added sugars, a lower %DV may help when comparing similar foods. For nutrients you want to get enough of, such as dietary fiber, a higher %DV may be useful. The %DV is a guide for each nutrient; the percentages are not meant to be added together into a score.

4. Notice sodium and other nutrients

The FDA identifies saturated fat, sodium and added sugars as nutrients to get less of. Dietary fiber, vitamin D, calcium, iron and potassium are nutrients many people should get more of.

For sodium, read both the amount in milligrams (mg) and its %DV. When comparing two similar products, check that their serving sizes are comparable. A high %DV means that one serving contributes a large share of the Daily Value; it does not, by itself, tell you everything about the food.

5. Separate total sugars from added sugars

Total Sugars includes sugars naturally present in foods such as milk and fruit, plus any added sugars in the product. Added Sugars includes sugars added during processing or preparation, including sugars from syrups and honey.

The word “includes” on the label means the added sugars are already counted within the total sugars amount. For example, a product could contain 15 g Total Sugars, including 7 g Added Sugars. Do not add those two numbers together.

Check the Added Sugars line and the ingredient list together. The ingredient list can show which sweeteners are used; this guide to five names for hidden sugars explains some common examples.

Worked example: what if you eat two servings?

The FDA's sample label for frozen lasagna lists one serving as 1 cup, with 280 calories and 850 mg sodium (37% DV). If someone eats 2 cups, that is two servings:

  • Calories: 280 × 2 = 560 calories.
  • Sodium: 850 mg × 2 = 1,700 mg.
  • Sodium %DV: 37% × 2 = 74% DV.

The lesson is simple: the numbers on the package only make sense when you know how many servings you actually eat. This is an illustration from the FDA's sample label, not a recommendation about portion size.

How to compare two similar products

  1. Compare the serving sizes first. Make sure you are not comparing a small serving with a much larger one.
  2. Check the nutrients that matter to your comparison, such as sodium, saturated fat, added sugars or dietary fiber.
  3. Use %DV as a quick low-or-high reference, then consider the ingredient list, product type, price and your own needs.

There is no single number that gives a complete verdict on a food. The label is a tool for understanding and comparing products, not a score that classifies every food as good or bad.

Common questions about the Nutrition Facts label

Is serving size the same as portion size?

No. Serving size is the standardized amount used to present the label information and is based on the amount people typically consume. Your portion is the amount you actually eat or drink.

What does 20% Daily Value mean?

It means one serving provides 20% of the Daily Value for that nutrient. Under the FDA's general guide, 20% DV or more is considered high for that nutrient.

Why does Total Sugars not always have a %DV?

The FDA has not established a Daily Value for Total Sugars. Total Sugars includes both naturally occurring sugars and Added Sugars; the label separately shows Added Sugars and its %DV.

Should I compare calories or %DV first?

Start with serving size. Then look at calories and the nutrients relevant to your comparison. %DV is especially useful for seeing whether a nutrient amount is relatively low or high per serving.

Does a Nutrition Facts label show the full ingredient list?

No. The Nutrition Facts panel shows amounts of calories and nutrients. The ingredient list names the ingredients used in the product. Read both when you want to understand a packaged food.
