Check the source. Read the context.

Food labels sit at the intersection of nutrition science, food technology and national regulations. These references are starting points for checking the claims explained on this site.

1. Healthy-diet guidance

The World Health Organization explains the principles of an adequate, balanced, moderate and diverse diet. Its guidance says free sugars should remain below 10% of total energy intake; reducing them to 5% or less may provide additional benefits. These are population-level recommendations, not an individual diet prescription.

WHO · Healthy diet fact sheet

2. Food additives and E-numbers

The European Food Safety Authority explains why additives are used, how they are identified on labels and how their safety is assessed and re-assessed. In the EU, an E-number identifies an authorised additive under specified conditions; it is not a danger rating. Authorisation and permitted conditions can differ by jurisdiction.

EFSA · Food additives · European Commission · Additives and EU rules

For titanium dioxide (E171), the regulatory position differs by jurisdiction: the EU withdrew its food-use authorisation from 7 August 2022; Northern Ireland follows EU food-additive rules; Great Britain retains authorisation; and the US FDA permits use under specified conditions. European Commission · Titanium dioxide withdrawal · UK Committee on Toxicity · E171 summary · Great Britain · E171 authorisation register · US FDA · Titanium dioxide in foods

3. Food information and nutrition labels in the EU

Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011 sets EU requirements for food information to consumers, including allergen presentation and nutrition information for most prepacked foods. It is an EU legal framework, not a worldwide labelling rule. Requirements in Morocco, the UK, the US and other markets must be checked against their own current authorities.

European Commission · Food information to consumers legislation · European Commission · Nutrition labelling

4. NOVA and food processing

NOVA is a research classification that groups foods by the extent and purpose of industrial processing. It can help describe processing patterns, but it is not a clinical diagnosis, a complete nutrient profile or a stand-alone verdict on an individual product. Read it alongside the ingredients, nutrition declaration and the role the food plays in the overall diet.

Monteiro et al. · “Ultra-processed foods: what they are and how to identify them” (2019) · Free full text at PubMed Central

5. Moroccan food-labelling rules

For products placed on the Moroccan market, start with the official ONSSA regulatory index. It lists Decree No. 2-12-389 on food-product labelling, as well as related texts covering ingredient designation, nutrition information on prepacked foods, food additives and specific product requirements. The framework is distinct from EU Regulation 1169/2011; do not apply EU requirements automatically to a Moroccan product.

ONSSA · Conformité des produits (official Moroccan regulatory index) · ONSSA · Food safety legislation, including Law No. 28-07

How to use this page: Sources are selected to help readers verify general explanations. A link to a regulator or scientific paper does not mean that every example on this site is an official recommendation. Scientific evidence and national rules can change; check the original source and the rules that apply where the product is sold.
