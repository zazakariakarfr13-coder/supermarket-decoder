E951: Aspartame

E951 is aspartame, a low-calorie sweetener about 200 times sweeter than sugar. It's used in diet drinks, sugar-free gum and yoghurts.

Why is E951 added to food?

Because it's so sweet, a tiny amount replaces a lot of sugar, with almost no calories. It breaks down with heat, so it's used in cold drinks and desserts rather than baking.

Where you'll find it

  • Diet and zero-sugar soft drinks
  • Sugar-free chewing gum and sweets
  • Light yoghurts and desserts
  • Tabletop sweeteners
  • Some medicines and vitamin tablets

Is E951 safe? What the research says

EFSA reviewed aspartame in 2013 and set an acceptable daily intake of 40 mg per kg of body weight. In 2023 the WHO's cancer agency (IARC) classed it as "possibly carcinogenic" (group 2B, the category for limited evidence), while the WHO/FAO expert committee kept the 40 mg limit. The WHO said a 70 kg adult would need to drink more than 9 to 14 cans of diet soda a day to go over it. People with phenylketonuria (PKU) must avoid it, which is why labels say "contains a source of phenylalanine".

An E-number is an identifier used in the European food-additive numbering system. It does not mean the additive is currently authorised in every country or food category. Rules differ by region and can change, so check local rules. The code is not a danger rating.

Questions people ask about E951

Does aspartame cause cancer?

In 2023 IARC classed it as "possibly carcinogenic" based on limited evidence, the same group as aloe vera extract. The WHO/FAO committee kept the safe intake limit unchanged. Research continues.

Why does my diet drink say "contains a source of phenylalanine"?

Aspartame contains phenylalanine, which people with the genetic condition PKU can't process. The warning is for them.

Is aspartame a sign of ultra-processing?

Yes. Sweeteners are one of the additive types NOVA uses as a marker of ultra-processed food.
